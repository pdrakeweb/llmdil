How an air-source heat pump heats a house in winter

An air-source heat pump does not make heat the way a furnace does.
A furnace burns fuel or runs current through a resistance element
and converts nearly all of that energy into heat inside the house.
A heat pump instead moves heat: it extracts thermal energy from
the outdoor air — air that feels cold to a person still contains
a great deal of thermal energy — and delivers that energy
indoors. Because the machine is transporting heat rather than
generating it, it can deliver two to four units of heat for every
unit of electricity it consumes. That ratio is called the
coefficient of performance, or COP. A resistance heater has a COP
of exactly 1. A heat pump at a COP of 3 delivers the same warmth
as a resistance heater while drawing a third of the power.

The mechanism is a refrigeration cycle, the same cycle a kitchen
refrigerator or an air conditioner runs, arranged so the useful
side is the indoor side. Four components do the work. The
compressor squeezes a refrigerant gas, raising its pressure and
temperature. The hot, high-pressure gas flows through a coil —
indoors, in heating mode — where a fan blows room air across it;
the refrigerant gives up heat to the room and condenses into a
liquid, which is why this coil is called the condenser when
heating. The liquid then passes through an expansion valve, which
drops its pressure abruptly, and with the pressure its
temperature, until the refrigerant is colder than the outdoor
air. In the outdoor coil — the evaporator, in heating mode — the
cold refrigerant absorbs heat from the outside air, boils back
into a gas, and returns to the compressor to repeat the cycle. A
reversing valve swaps the roles of the two coils for summer
cooling, which is why one machine can both heat and cool.

Why capacity falls as the weather gets colder is a direct
consequence of the physics. Colder outdoor air carries less
extractable heat per cubic metre, and the compressor must work
across a wider temperature lift — from a colder evaporating
temperature to the same warm condensing temperature — which
reduces both the mass of refrigerant it can move effectively and
the COP it can achieve. A typical modern unit rated at 36,000
BTU per hour at 8 °C outdoors might deliver only about 22,000 to
26,000 BTU per hour at −12 °C, at a COP near 2 instead of 3.5.
Cold-climate models fight this with larger outdoor coils, vapor
injection compressors that squeeze in two stages, and controls
that keep the compressor running continuously at variable speed
rather than cycling, and they hold useful capacity down to
−20 °C or below — but no air-source machine escapes the trend,
only softens it.

Because capacity falls with temperature while a house's heat loss
rises with temperature difference, the two curves cross. Below
that crossover, called the balance point, the heat pump alone
cannot hold the indoor temperature, and a backup heat source
takes up the difference: electric resistance strips, called
auxiliary heat, or a gas furnace in a dual-fuel arrangement.
Good controls stage the backup in only as far as needed and only
below the balance point, because every unit of heat from
resistance backup arrives at a COP of 1; a system that calls for
auxiliary heat too eagerly gives back much of the efficiency the
heat pump was bought for. The economic balance point — where
heat-pump heat costs the same per unit as the backup fuel — is a
separate figure that depends on electricity and gas prices, and
it, not the thermal balance point, decides the switchover in a
dual-fuel system.

Frost is the other winter complication. The outdoor coil runs
colder than the outdoor air, so moisture from the air freezes on
it, insulating it and blocking airflow. The machine periodically
runs a defrost cycle: it reverses briefly into cooling mode,
sending hot refrigerant to the outdoor coil to melt the frost,
while auxiliary heat tempers the cool air this would otherwise
blow indoors. Defrost cycles are normal, brief, and more frequent
in humid air near freezing than in colder, drier air — the
frostiest conditions are around −2 °C to +3 °C with high
humidity, not the coldest nights of the year. Steam rising from
the outdoor unit during defrost is meltwater evaporating, not a
fault, though a unit that defrosts every few minutes for long
stretches, or never defrosts and ices solid, needs service.

Sizing and installation decide more of a system's real
performance than the nameplate does. An oversized single-stage
unit short-cycles in mild weather, wearing the compressor and
dehumidifying poorly in summer; an undersized one leans on
backup heat all winter. Ductwork sized for an old furnace's hot,
fast air may deliver a heat pump's larger volume of gentler air
noisily or unevenly, and refrigerant charge, airflow across the
indoor coil, and thermostat configuration each move delivered
COP by amounts homeowners can feel on a bill. This is why two
houses with identical outdoor units can report very different
winter electricity use: the machine is one component of a system
whose other components are the ducts, the envelope, and the
controls.

The envelope belongs in the same discussion. Every improvement
in insulation and air sealing lowers the house's heat-loss curve,
which pushes the balance point to a colder temperature, shrinks
the hours the backup heat runs, and can let a smaller, cheaper
heat pump serve the house. In that sense a heat pump purchase
and an attic-insulation purchase are not competing uses of the
same money so much as two halves of one decision, and the order
matters: reduce the load first, then size the machine to the
reduced load.
